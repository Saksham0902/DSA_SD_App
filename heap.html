    How a heap works

    A complete binary tree stored in a plain list, where every parent beats its children. That's enough to give you the best item in O(1) and update in O(log n).

    A tree hiding in a list

    Store the tree level by level in an array. Node i has children 2i + 1 and 2i + 2, and parent (i − 1) // 2. No pointers needed.

    The only rule (min-heap): every parent ≤ its children. So the root is the minimum. To push, add at the end and sift up while you're smaller than your parent. To pop, move the last leaf to the root and sift down toward the smaller child. Both walk one root-to-leaf path: O(log n).

    Top K, merging, medians

    Keep only what matters, always pull the best available, and balance two halves.

    The bouncer

    To keep the k largest of a stream, keep a min-heap of size k. Its root is the weakest of your top k, like a bouncer at the door. Every newcomer is compared with the bouncer: if it's bigger, the bouncer is thrown out. At the end, the root is exactly the kth largest.

    Counting the cost

    Everything costs the height of the tree, and the height is log n.

    How to price a heap solution

    1. A heap is a complete binary tree, so its height is ⌊log₂ n⌋. Push and pop sift along one root-to-leaf path → O(log n). Peeking at the top is O(1).
    2. Multiply by how many pushes and pops you do. n pushes + n pops → O(n log n).
    3. Keep the heap small when you can. Top-k with a heap capped at size k costs O(n log k), cheaper than sorting all n (O(n log n)).
    4. heapify on an existing list is O(n), not O(n log n): most nodes are near the bottom and barely move.
